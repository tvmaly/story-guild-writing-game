# Tiny Tales: A Third-Grade Course in 100-Word Stories

**For:** an 8-year-old homeschool student  
**Level:** average third-grade reading and writing  
**Length:** 4 weeks, 12 lessons  
**Lesson time:** about 20–35 minutes  
**Final project:** one polished, illustrated story of exactly 100 words plus one shorter companion version

> **Big idea:** A tiny story can still have a character, a problem, action, and an ending. The word limit turns writing into a puzzle: choose the words that do the most work.

---

## About This Adaptation

This course adapts Laura Gibbs's approach to 100-word stories, also called **drabbles**, for a younger homeschool writer. It keeps the main ideas of the original approach:

- write a complete story in a small space;
- make every word count;
- try the same idea at different lengths;
- get ideas from old stories, pictures, daily life, and imagination;
- change a story's plot, characters, point of view, setting, or style;
- give the story a useful title;
- revise, count words, add an illustration, and explain creative choices.

The activities below add drawing, toys, movement, dice, cards, building materials, oral storytelling, and simple “story debugging.”

---

# Parent Guide

## Course Goals

By the end of the course, the student should be able to:

1. Tell the difference between a description and a story.
2. Build a story around a character who wants something.
3. Include a problem, an attempt, and a result.
4. Put events in a clear order.
5. Use a few strong verbs, sensory details, and lines of dialogue.
6. Retell or remix a familiar story in a new way.
7. Write the same idea in 6, 25, 50, and 100 words.
8. Revise a draft by cutting, adding, moving, and replacing words.
9. Use capitals, end marks, paragraph breaks, and readable spelling.
10. Publish and read aloud an illustrated 100-word story.

## Recommended Schedule

| Week | Lessons | Main focus |
|---|---:|---|
| 1 | 1–3 | What makes a tiny story; finding ideas; character goals and problems |
| 2 | 4–6 | Beginning-middle-end; vivid words; dialogue |
| 3 | 7–9 | Retelling and remixing; shorter forms; first long draft |
| 4 | 10–12 | Cutting to 100 words; editing; publishing and sharing |

The schedule is flexible. A child may do one lesson per day, three lessons per week, or repeat a favorite lesson before moving on.

## Materials

Use what is already available. Helpful materials include:

- index cards or small pieces of paper;
- pencils, colored pencils, crayons, and markers;
- six-sided dice;
- sticky notes;
- scissors and tape;
- toy figures, blocks, LEGO bricks, or small household objects;
- 100 small counters, such as buttons, dry beans, coins, blocks, or paper squares;
- a paper plate and paper clip for a spinner;
- a timer or stopwatch;
- optional voice recorder, tablet, or word processor;
- a folder or binder for finished work.

## How to Help Without Taking Over

- Let the child **tell the story aloud** before writing it.
- A parent may write down the child's spoken words during early lessons.
- Speech-to-text is acceptable for a child who has more ideas than handwriting stamina.
- During the first draft, focus on the story. Save spelling corrections for later.
- Ask questions instead of supplying sentences: “What does he want?” “What goes wrong?” “What happens because of that?”
- Offer two choices when the child is stuck.
- Stop while the lesson is still enjoyable. A polished story can take several short sessions.
- Treat exactly 100 words as a **word puzzle**, not a test of intelligence.

## A Simple Lesson Rhythm

The parent may read or paraphrase the directions. The child is not expected to read this whole course alone. Student tasks use short sentences and are meant to be completed one small section at a time.

Most lessons follow this pattern:

1. **Talk — 3 to 5 minutes:** Read or discuss a tiny example.
2. **Play — 5 to 10 minutes:** Use toys, cards, drawing, movement, or dice.
3. **Write — 10 to 15 minutes:** Complete a small writing task.
4. **Share — 2 to 5 minutes:** Read aloud and name one successful choice.

## Word-Count Rules for This Course

Use the same rules throughout the course so counting does not become confusing.

1. The **title does not count** as part of the story.
2. A word separated by spaces usually counts as one word.
3. A contraction such as *can't* counts as one word.
4. A number such as *12* counts as one word.
5. Use one word-count tool for the final story and follow its result.
6. Count only the story, not the author's note.

For early drafts, an adult can count the words. During revision, the child can use counters: move one counter for each word, placing them into groups of ten.

## What “Complete Story” Means

Use this child-friendly pattern:

> **Somebody wanted something, but a problem happened, so the character acted, and then something changed.**

Not every story must follow this exact sentence pattern, but it is a useful test.

# Model Story: One Idea at Four Lengths

The title is not part of the word count.

## 100 Words: “Bolt and the Rain”

> Max built a small robot from an old lunch box. He named it Bolt. On Tuesday, dark clouds covered the yard. “Let’s race!” Max said. Bolt rolled outside, but one raindrop hit his metal head. He froze. Another drop fell. Bolt beeped and hid under a chair. Max made a tiny umbrella from a paper plate and a stick. Together, they crossed the wet grass. At the finish line, Bolt found a muddy puppy shaking all alone beside the fence. He held the umbrella over it. Bolt still disliked rain, but now he knew he could be brave in it.

**Word count: 100**

## 50 Words

> Max's robot Bolt feared rain. When clouds opened above the yard, Bolt hid under a chair. Max gave him a tiny umbrella. At the finish line, they found a muddy puppy all alone by the fence. Bolt covered it. He still feared rain, but he rolled through the storm anyway.

**Word count: 50**

## 25 Words

> Bolt feared rain, but a muddy puppy needed help. With Max's tiny umbrella, the robot crossed the yard and learned he was brave after all.

**Word count: 25**

## 6 Words

> Rain scared Bolt. Kindness moved him.

**Word count: 6**

### Talk About It

- What stayed the same in every version?
- What disappeared as the stories became shorter?
- Which version gives the clearest picture?
- Which version makes the reader imagine the most?
- Which title would help the six-word version most?

# Lesson 1: A Whole Story in a Tiny Space

## Student Goal

**I can tell whether a group of words is a story.**

## Time

20–25 minutes

## Materials

- two small boxes or containers, one large and one small;
- five to ten small objects;
- index cards;
- pencil.

## Talk

A story is not only a list of facts. Something happens, and something changes.

Compare:

- **Description:** A silver robot stood beside a tree.
- **Story:** A silver robot lost its key, searched the tree, and woke a sleeping owl.

The second example feels like a story because an event changes the situation.

## Hands-On Activity: The Tiny Box Challenge

1. Place several objects in a large box.
2. Try to move all of them into the smaller box.
3. When they do not fit, choose only the most useful objects.
4. Explain the connection: a 100-word story is a small box. A writer keeps the most useful words.

## Story-or-Not Card Sort

Copy each item onto a card. Mix the cards. Sort them into **STORY** and **NOT YET A STORY**.

1. A green dragon slept under a bridge.
2. A green dragon sneezed fire and burned his own map.
3. The goalie wore red shoes and blue gloves.
4. The goalie missed the ball, chased it downhill, and saved a duck.
5. A drum sat in the attic.
6. Mia tapped the old drum, and a hidden door opened.

Ask: “What changed in each story?”

## Write

Finish this pattern in one sentence:

> A __________ wanted __________, but __________, so __________, and then __________.

Now turn the sentence into a story of **six to twelve words**. Do not worry about making it exactly six words yet.

## Share

Read the tiny story aloud. The listener answers:

- Who is it about?
- What happened?
- What changed?

## Optional Movement Game

Act out a beginning pose, a problem pose, and an ending pose. A parent guesses the story.

# Lesson 2: Build a Story Seed

## Student Goal

**I can make a story idea from a character, place, goal, and problem.**

## Time

25–30 minutes

## Materials

- four cups or envelopes;
- small paper slips;
- toy figures or blocks;
- optional die.

## Hands-On Activity: Four-Cup Story Machine

Label four cups:

1. **WHO?**
2. **WHERE?**
3. **WANTS WHAT?**
4. **WHAT GOES WRONG?**

Write or draw choices on slips. Put them in the matching cups. Pull one slip from each cup.

### Sample Slips

| WHO? | WHERE? | WANTS WHAT? | WHAT GOES WRONG? |
|---|---|---|---|
| a robot | an attic | find a missing key | the lights go out |
| a drummer | a jungle | win a contest | the drum talks |
| a young detective | a stadium | solve a mystery | the map is upside down |
| a dog | a space station | help a friend | the door is locked |
| a chess knight | a game world | return home | the bridge disappears |
| an inventor | a beach | test a machine | a storm begins |

## Play the Story First

Use toys or blocks to act out a 30-second story from the four slips. The story may be silly. It only needs to make sense.

## Plan

Complete the five parts:

- **Somebody:** ____________________________________
- **Wanted:** ______________________________________
- **But:** _________________________________________
- **So:** __________________________________________
- **Then:** ________________________________________

## Write

Write three to five sentences. Use the plan, but change anything that improves the story.

## Share

The listener gives one response:

> “My favorite part was __________________ because __________________.”

## Optional Challenge: Roll a Story

Roll one die for each column in the “Roll-a-Story Tables” near the end of this course.

# Lesson 3: Give the Character a Want and a Problem

## Student Goal

**I can create a character who wants something and must solve a problem.**

## Time

25–30 minutes

## Materials

- one toy, puppet, or drawn character;
- blocks, cups, books, or pillows;
- character interview sheet.

## Hands-On Activity 1: Interview a Character

Pretend the toy or puppet can answer questions. The child may speak for the character while the parent asks:

1. What is your name?
2. What do you want today?
3. Why does it matter to you?
4. What are you good at?
5. What are you afraid of?
6. What could stop you?

Record short answers.

## Hands-On Activity 2: Character Obstacle Course

Build a tabletop path with three obstacles. Move the character through it.

- Obstacle 1 should be easy.
- Obstacle 2 should force the character to try a new idea.
- Obstacle 3 should lead to the ending.

Tell the story aloud while moving the figure.

## Write

Use this frame only if needed:

> __________ wanted __________ because __________. First, __________. But __________. The character tried __________. At last, __________.

Write four to seven sentences.

## Quick Check

Circle **yes** or **not yet**:

- I know what the character wants. — **yes / not yet**
- A problem makes the goal difficult. — **yes / not yet**
- The character does something about the problem. — **yes / not yet**
- The ending shows what happened. — **yes / not yet**

## Optional Challenge

Give the character a useful weakness or an unexpected strength. For example, a robot may fear water but be very good at finding lost animals.

# Lesson 4: Build a Beginning, Middle, and End

## Student Goal

**I can place story events in a clear order.**

## Time

25–35 minutes

## Materials

- three index cards;
- crayons or markers;
- tape;
- toy figure.

## Hands-On Activity: Three-Card Movie

Label three cards:

1. **BEGINNING — Want**
2. **MIDDLE — Trouble and Try**
3. **ENDING — Result and Change**

Draw one scene on each card. Lay the cards in order. Move a toy from card to card while telling the story.

Then shuffle the cards. Put them back in the clearest order and explain why.

## Useful Time Words

Choose only the ones that help:

- At first
- One morning
- Suddenly
- Next
- But
- Because
- So
- A moment later
- At last
- In the end

## Write

Turn the three cards into six to eight sentences. Ignore the 100-word limit for now.

## Story Test

Cover the middle card. Does the story still make sense? If it does, the middle may need a stronger problem or more important action.

Cover the ending card. Is something missing? A good ending should show a result or change.

## Optional Movement Activity

Place the three cards across the room. Walk or hop to each card and tell that part of the story without looking at notes.

# Lesson 5: Use Words That Make a Picture

## Student Goal

**I can choose strong verbs and a few useful sensory details.**

## Time

25–30 minutes

## Materials

- paper and pencil;
- small bag;
- three safe household objects;
- optional drum or rhythm instrument.

## Hands-On Activity 1: Sensory Detective

Place an object in a bag without showing it. The child reaches in and describes it without naming it.

Ask:

- What does it feel like?
- Does it make a sound?
- Does it have a smell?
- What size and shape is it?
- What does it remind you of?

Choose the **one or two most useful details**. Tiny stories do not have room for every detail.

## Hands-On Activity 2: Verb Charades

Act out one verb while the other person guesses:

- walked, marched, crept, raced, stumbled;
- looked, stared, peeked, searched, spotted;
- said, whispered, shouted, groaned, cheered;
- held, grabbed, carried, dragged, tossed.

Discuss how a strong verb can replace several weaker words.

## Replace Weak Phrases

| Longer or weaker phrase | Stronger choice |
|---|---|
| went very fast | raced |
| said very quietly | whispered |
| looked for a long time | searched |
| made a loud sound | crashed |
| was very scared | trembled |
| moved in a slow, careful way | crept |

## Write

Return to the story from Lesson 4.

1. Underline three verbs.
2. Replace at least one with a stronger verb.
3. Add one sound, sight, smell, taste, or touch detail.
4. Remove any detail that does not help the action or mood.

## Optional Soundtrack

Use a drum, table tap, or hand clap to create three sounds: beginning, danger, and ending. Read the story with the sounds.

# Lesson 6: Let the Characters Talk

## Student Goal

**I can use a short line of dialogue that changes the story.**

## Time

25–30 minutes

## Materials

- two puppets, figures, or paper characters;
- two speech-bubble cards;
- pencil.

## Talk

Dialogue earns its place when it does at least one job:

- shows what a character wants;
- reveals a clue;
- starts a problem;
- shows a feeling;
- causes the next action.

“Hello. How are you?” often uses words without moving a tiny story forward.

## Hands-On Activity: Puppet Problem Talk

Give two characters a problem. Act out a short conversation, using no more than three lines.

Example:

> “The bridge is gone,” said Leo.  
> “Then we build one,” said Ana.  
> The dragon below opened one eye.

Write each spoken line inside a paper speech bubble. Move the bubbles next to the correct figures.

## Dialogue Punctuation

Use this simple pattern:

> “Spoken words,” said the character.

A question uses a question mark:

> “Where is the key?” asked Max.

An excited or shouted line may use an exclamation point:

> “Run!” shouted Mia.

## Write

Add **one or two short lines** of dialogue to an earlier story. Read the story without the dialogue and then with it. Keep the dialogue only if it improves the story.

## Optional Challenge

Write a two-sentence story in which one sentence is dialogue.

# Lesson 7: Retell and Remix

## Student Goal

**I can take a known story and change it in a new way.**

## Time

30–35 minutes

## Materials

- a familiar fable, fairy tale, family story, or earlier student story;
- paper plate, pencil, and paper clip;
- paper and pencil.

## Talk

Writers often get ideas by retelling old stories. A retelling should add a new choice. It should not merely copy the original.

## Hands-On Activity: Make a Remix Spinner

Divide a paper plate into six sections. Write:

1. Change the **main character**.
2. Change the **setting**.
3. Change the **problem or ending**.
4. Tell it from a **different point of view**.
5. Change the **style**: diary, news report, text messages, instructions, song, or comic.
6. Tell what happened **before or after** the known story.

Place a paper clip at the center. Hold it with a pencil tip and flick it to spin.

## Choose a Source Story

Good starting points include:

- The Tortoise and the Hare;
- The Lion and the Mouse;
- The Boy Who Cried Wolf;
- The Three Little Pigs;
- a family memory;
- a book read during homeschool;
- one of the student's own earlier stories.

## Plan the Change

- Original story: ___________________________________
- My remix choice: _________________________________
- What will stay the same? __________________________
- What will change? ________________________________

## Play and Write

Act out the remix with figures or make a three-panel comic. Then write five to eight sentences.

## Give Credit

Under the story, write a simple source note:

> Inspired by “The Lion and the Mouse,” an Aesop fable.

## Optional Challenges

- Tell the story from the “villain's” point of view.
- Put the story in a video-game world, sports arena, space station, or workshop.
- Change the ending but keep the original lesson.
- Keep the ending but change the lesson.

# Lesson 8: One Story, Many Sizes

## Student Goal

**I can keep the heart of a story while making it shorter.**

## Time

30–35 minutes

## Materials

- the four “Bolt and the Rain” examples;
- four nesting boxes, cups, or paper rectangles;
- sticky notes;
- pencil.

## Hands-On Activity: Nesting Story Boxes

Label the containers or rectangles:

- 100 words
- 50 words
- 25 words
- 6 words

On sticky notes, write or draw important parts of a familiar story. Decide which details fit in every box and which fit only in the larger boxes.

The smallest box should still hold the **heart** of the story: the main change, discovery, choice, or surprise.

## Find the Heart

Complete this sentence:

> This story is really about __________________________________________.

Examples:

- being brave even while afraid;
- helping someone smaller;
- solving a problem by thinking differently;
- learning that winning is not everything.

## Write Two Sizes

Choose an earlier story.

1. Write a version of **about 50 words**.
2. Write a version of **25 words**.
3. Give each version a title. The titles do not count.

The parent may help count.

## Reflect

- What detail was hardest to remove?
- What idea had to stay?
- Did the shorter version make the reader fill in more gaps?

## Optional Six-Word Challenge

Write the story in exactly six words. A six-word story may feel like a clue, snapshot, or riddle.

Useful pattern:

> **Problem. Choice. Result.**

Example:

> Final shot missed. Teammates still cheered.

# Lesson 9: Draft Big Before Cutting Small

## Student Goal

**I can tell the full story first and worry about 100 words later.**

## Time

30–35 minutes, possibly split across two days

## Materials

- story seed or remix plan;
- toys, cards, or storyboard;
- paper or word processor;
- optional voice recorder;
- 100 counters.

## Step 1: Build or Act It Out

Create the scene with blocks and figures. Tell the story aloud from start to finish.

A parent may record the oral story. The student listens once and names the best part.

## Step 2: Make a Six-Part Plan

1. Character and setting
2. What the character wants
3. Problem
4. First attempt
5. Important action or decision
6. Result and change

## Step 3: Write Freely

Write **8 to 12 sentences**. A first draft between about **120 and 180 words** is fine. Do not stop after every sentence to count or correct.

A parent may:

- write down dictated sentences;
- type while the student talks;
- let the student use speech-to-text;
- take turns writing sentences.

## Step 4: Count Without Judging

Record the count:

> First draft: __________ words

Say:

> “Now we know the size. Next time we will solve the 100-word puzzle.”

## Hands-On Word Count

Move one counter for every word. Make ten groups of ten. Stop at 100 and mark where the extra words begin.

Do not cut yet. Simply notice:

- Which part used the most words?
- Where does the action really begin?
- Is the ending too short or too long?

# Lesson 10: Word Surgery — Cut to 100

## Student Goal

**I can remove or replace words without hurting the story.**

## Time

30–40 minutes, possibly split across two days

## Materials

- a copy of the first draft with wide spacing;
- scissors;
- three colored pencils or markers;
- tape;
- word-count tool or counters.

## Hands-On Activity 1: Sentence Surgery

Copy or print the draft. Cut it into sentence strips.

Mark each strip:

- **Green — Must keep:** needed to understand the story.
- **Yellow — May shorten:** useful, but too long.
- **Red — May cut:** repeats something or does not change the story.

Move the strips until the order is clear. Tape them onto a new page.

## Five Safe Ways to Cut

### 1. Remove repeated information

> The small, tiny robot was little.  
> **Better:** The tiny robot...

### 2. Replace a phrase with one strong word

> ran as fast as he could  
> **Better:** sprinted

### 3. Begin closer to the action

> It was a normal sunny morning, and Max had eaten breakfast and put on his shoes before he went outside.  
> **Better:** Max raced outside.

### 4. Remove filler words

Words such as *really, very, just, suddenly,* and *then* are sometimes useful, but often removable.

### 5. Combine sentences

> The dog was wet. The dog shook beside the fence.  
> **Better:** The wet dog shook beside the fence.

## Hands-On Activity 2: Pay with Word Tokens

Give the child 100 counters. Each word costs one counter. When the counters are gone, the story is full.

For every new word added, another word must leave.

## If the Story Is Under 100 Words

Add words that help the reader, not empty words. Consider adding:

- a clearer goal;
- the cause of the problem;
- one important action;
- one sensory detail;
- a short line of dialogue;
- a stronger ending.

## Story Debugging Checklist

Think like a programmer testing a project:

- [ ] **Input:** Do I introduce the character and situation?
- [ ] **Goal:** Does the character want something?
- [ ] **Bug:** Is there a problem or surprise?
- [ ] **Action:** Does the character make a choice or try something?
- [ ] **Output:** Does the ending show a result or change?
- [ ] **Limit:** Does the tool say exactly 100 words?

## Save Both Versions

Keep the first draft and the 100-word draft. Seeing both versions makes the revision work visible.

# Lesson 11: Make Every Word Work

## Student Goal

**I can improve clarity, sound, spelling, and punctuation.**

## Time

25–35 minutes

## Materials

- 100-word draft;
- colored pencils;
- feedback card;
- word-count tool.

## Step 1: Read Aloud

The child reads the story slowly. The parent follows with a finger but does not interrupt.

Mark any place where the reader:

- runs out of breath;
- becomes confused;
- repeats a word by accident;
- trips over a sentence;
- wants a stronger ending.

## Step 2: Listener Feedback

The listener completes only these three statements:

1. **I pictured** ___________________________________.
2. **I wondered** __________________________________.
3. **My favorite word or moment was** _______________.

The writer chooses what to change. Not every suggestion must be used.

## Step 3: Four-Color Edit

Use one color for each job:

1. Circle capital letters at the start of sentences.
2. Box end punctuation.
3. Underline dialogue punctuation.
4. Star words whose spelling should be checked.

Do not circle every invented name or difficult word. Check only the words in this story.

## Step 4: Recount

Editing can change the word count. Confirm that the final story still has exactly 100 words.

## Step 5: Choose a Working Title

A title can:

- name the mystery;
- hint at the problem;
- create a question;
- add information that is not in the story;
- take on a new meaning after the ending.

Possible title patterns:

- **The Last __________**
- **When __________ Spoke**
- **The Mystery of __________**
- **One More __________**
- **Why __________ Would Not __________**

## Step 6: Write an Author's Note

The author's note does not count toward 100 words. Write two to four sentences:

> I got my idea from __________________________________.  
> I changed __________________________________________.  
> The hardest part was _______________________________.  
> My favorite choice was _____________________________.

# Lesson 12: Publish and Celebrate

## Student Goal

**I can present my story so another person can enjoy it.**

## Time

30–45 minutes

## Materials

- final story;
- paper, cardstock, or folder;
- art supplies;
- optional phone or tablet for audio;
- optional building materials.

## Choose One Publishing Project

### Option A: Illustrated Story Page

Place the title at the top, the story in the middle, and an illustration below or around it. Add the author's note on the back.

### Option B: Accordion Book

1. Fold a long sheet of paper into four panels.
2. Put the title and author on panel 1.
3. Divide the story across panels 2 and 3.
4. Put the ending, picture, and author's note on panel 4.

### Option C: Story Scene

Build one important scene with blocks, recycled materials, clay, or toys. Place the printed story beside it.

### Option D: Audio Tiny Tale

Record a clear reading. Add safe homemade sound effects. The recording should support the words rather than cover them.

### Option E: Tiny Story Museum

Display:

- the 100-word story and one 50-, 25-, or 6-word companion version;
- the first plan;
- the long first draft;
- the sentence strips;
- the final 100-word story;
- the illustration or model.

This shows that writing is made through choices and revision.

## Read-Aloud Celebration

Before reading:

1. Take one slow breath.
2. Hold the page still.
3. Read loudly enough to hear.
4. Pause at punctuation.
5. Look up once or twice.

After reading, the listener asks one friendly question about the story world.

## Final Reflection

Complete:

- I learned that tiny stories ________________________________________.
- I am proud of __________________________________________________.
- The best word in my story is __________________ because ______________.
- Next time I want to write a story about ______________________________.

# Hands-On Story Games

These may replace a lesson warm-up or provide extra practice.

## 1. Ten-Word Relay

Take turns adding exactly ten words to a shared story. Complete ten turns for a 100-word story. Read the result and revise it together so it makes sense.

## 2. Story Debugger

Build a quick story with figures. The parent secretly removes one part: the goal, problem, action, or ending. The child identifies the “missing code” and repairs the story.

## 3. Mystery Object

Hide an object in a bag. The child studies it by touch and writes a story in which the object becomes an important clue.

## 4. Freeze-Frame Story

Make three poses for beginning, middle, and end. Photograph or sketch each pose. Write one or two sentences under each picture.

## 5. Story Sound Lab

Choose three sounds with a drum, tapping objects, clapping, or voice effects. Decide what event each sound represents. Tell a story that uses the sounds in order.

## 6. Build, Break, Fix

Build a small structure. Give the character a reason to cross, enter, protect, or repair it. Change one piece to create a problem. Act out the solution and write the story.

## 7. Forbidden Words

Choose three common words that may not appear, such as *went, said,* and *very*. The writer must find stronger replacements.

## 8. Backward Story

Act out the ending first. Ask, “What must have happened just before this?” Continue moving backward until the beginning is found. Then write the story in normal order.

## 9. Picture Walk

Take a short walk and photograph or sketch three interesting things. Combine all three into one tiny story.

## 10. Stop-Motion Plan

Build five still scenes with toys. Take one photo of each. Arrange them in order and write one sentence per scene. Then combine and revise the sentences.

# Optional Mini-Units

## A. The 100-Word Mystery

A very short mystery needs only four parts:

1. a puzzling event;
2. two or three clues;
3. a reasonable guess;
4. a solution or final twist.

### Hands-On Clue Board

Place three objects on a tray. Secretly choose one as the key clue. The child creates a mystery in which all three appear but only one solves the case.

## B. The 100-Word Sports Story

Focus on a choice, not a full game. Begin near the important moment:

- the last play;
- a mistake;
- a surprising act of teamwork;
- a fair choice that costs a win;
- helping an injured rival.

### Hands-On Play Diagram

Use coins or blocks as players. Move them through one important play, then tell what the main player notices, decides, and does.

## C. The 100-Word Invention Story

Use this pattern:

> A character builds something for one purpose. It fails in an interesting way. That failure solves a different problem.

### Hands-On Prototype

Build a harmless model from blocks, cardboard, tape, or recycled parts. Give it one button, lever, wheel, sensor, or silly flaw. Demonstrate it before writing.

## D. Ten Sentences of Ten Words

Write ten sentences with exactly ten words each. This automatically makes 100 words.

This is a math-and-writing challenge, not a requirement. Sentences should still sound natural. Revise any sentence that feels padded merely to reach ten.

## E. Two Points of View

Write the same event twice:

- 50 words from Character A;
- 50 words from Character B.

Together, the two views make 100 words. Let the characters notice different things.

# Parent Assessment

Use this only after the final story is complete. The purpose is to choose the next teaching step, not to reduce the story to a score.

| Skill | Starting | Growing | Ready to Share |
|---|---|---|---|
| Story shape | Events are mostly a list or description. | Character, problem, and ending are present but one part needs clarity. | Character wants something, faces trouble, acts, and reaches a clear result. |
| Order | Events are hard to follow. | Most events are in order. | Time and cause are easy to follow. |
| Word choice | Many general or repeated words. | Some strong verbs or helpful details. | Words are specific, concise, and easy to picture. |
| Revision | Needs adult direction for each change. | Can choose among suggested changes. | Independently cuts, adds, moves, or replaces words for a reason. |
| Conventions | Errors often block understanding. | Some errors remain, but meaning is clear. | Capitals, punctuation, and spelling make the story easy to read. |
| Word limit | Count is far from 100 or inconsistent. | Reaches 100 with substantial support. | Reaches exactly 100 and can explain revision choices. |

## Suggested Next Steps

- If story shape is weak, repeat Lessons 2–4 with toys and oral storytelling.
- If handwriting blocks ideas, use dictation and let the child revise the typed text.
- If the story is clear but wordy, repeat Lesson 8 with 50- and 25-word versions.
- If reaching 100 causes padding, ask what information the reader truly needs.
- If revision is strong, try a new point of view, genre, or 100-word structure.
